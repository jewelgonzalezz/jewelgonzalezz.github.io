World Life Expectancy & Healthcare Spending Overview

An exploratory data analysis examining the relationship between healthcare spending and life expectancy across countries from 2000 to 2023. The project investigates whether higher spending is associated with longer lives and where that relationship begins to break down.

01 / OVERVIEW

The question behind the data.

Healthcare spending is often discussed as an important factor in population health. But spending more does not necessarily mean getting proportionally better outcomes. This project compares spending levels with life expectancy to explore the patterns, differences, and exceptions across countries and regions.

Does spending more on healthcare lead to longer lives, and where does that relationship break down?

Tools

Power BI, Power Query, and DAX.

Data source

Our World in Data (OWID).

Time period

2000–2023.

02 / KEY FINDINGS

What the data revealed.

The dashboard compares healthcare spending and life expectancy across spending tiers, countries, and regions.

01

Diminishing returns on spending

Average life expectancy was 72.3 years in the low-spending tier, 77.2 years in the medium tier, and 81.0 years in the high-spending tier. The difference between the medium and high tiers was under four years despite roughly $3,400 more in spending per person. These are observed associations, not proof of causation.

02

The United States stands out

The US spends nearly $10,000 per person on healthcare in this analysis, yet its life expectancy is lower than that of several countries spending considerably less.

03

Higher life expectancy at lower spending

Japan and Malta are examples of countries achieving high life expectancy at lower spending levels than the US or Switzerland in the comparison presented.

04

Regional disparities remain visible

The dashboard highlights a substantial life expectancy gap in Africa, alongside improvements since approximately 2005.

05

The impact of 2020

Life expectancy trends show a dip around 2020 across most regions, consistent with the widespread disruption associated with the COVID-19 pandemic.
